Abstract

012

The SLAM-ASR recipe — a frozen speech encoder, a frozen large language model as the decoder, and a small trainable projector between
them — is a practical route for adding lowresource languages to speech systems, but the
choice of decoder within it has not been studied
under controlled conditions. We ask whether
pairing each language with a regionally specialized decoder improves recognition, using
the Tiny Aya family, whose four variants share
one base model and one tokenizer and differ
only in the regional weighting of their posttraining data. Across twelve languages, the
region-matched decoder is only 0.55 character
error rate (CER) better on average than mismatched regional decoders (Wilcoxon signedrank p = 0.97). This absence of a matcheddecoder advantage persists on held-out test sets,
where the difference is −0.09 CER against a
minimum detectable effect of 0.67. The advantage also does not grow with the amount
of target-language data the decoder saw: for
the median language, the matched variant saw
just 1.3 percentage points more target-language
data than the mismatched ones — at most 5.7 —
though up to 236 times more in relative terms,
and recognition accuracy tracks neither measure. What does matter is training the projector at all: on low-resource languages, it improves CER over off-the-shelf models by up
to 102.6, including a language supported by
neither frozen component. For practitioners, a
general-purpose multilingual decoder suffices,
and the effort is better spent on in-language
audio. We will release all checkpoints.

ASR (team et al., 2025) have expanded multilingual speech recognition to a growing number of languages. Despite this progress, substantial work remains on the breadth and diversity of low-resource
languages and dialects, which has motivated recent
studies of the failure modes of these systems in
low-resource settings (Imam et al., 2025b; Kyslyi
et al., 2026; Kumar et al., 2026; Bharathi et al.,
2026; Azis et al., 2026).
One recent direction addressing these constraints
is the SLAM-ASR architecture (Ma et al., 2024),
which connects a pretrained speech encoder to a
large language model (LLM) through a trainable
linear projector while keeping both pretrained models frozen. Follow-up work has probed the recipe’s
robustness (Kumar et al., 2025), its data requirements (Fong et al., 2025), encoder depth (Kolluri et al., 2026), language-family effects (Zhang
et al., 2026), and prompt sensitivity (Burdisso et al.,
2026). However, the effect of the decoder’s regional specialization has not been studied. The
Tiny Aya family (Salamanca et al., 2026) makes the
missing experiment possible: small multilingual
LLMs sharing a common base model and tokenizer,
where each regional variant differs only in the posttraining data reinforcing its region’s languages.

low-resource languages, decoder specialization,
Tiny Aya

Method

Our implementation follows the Encoder–
Projector–Decoder architecture of SLAM-ASR
(Ma et al., 2024), illustrated in Figure 1. A frozen
encoder extracts acoustic representations from
the input speech; a trainable linear projector
downsamples and maps them into the decoder’s
embedding space; the frozen LLM decoder
autoregressively generates the transcription. Only
the projector is optimized, which learns the
alignment between speech representations and the
language-model embedding space with a minimal
number of trainable parameters.

Related Work

Projector-based ASR. Ma et al. (2024) showed
that a single trainable linear projector between a
frozen speech encoder and a frozen LLM reaches
competitive English recognition, and subsequent
work has probed its robustness (Kumar et al., 2025),
how much labelled audio it needs (Fong et al.,
2025), how far the encoder can be pruned (Kolluri et al., 2026), how language-family structure
affects it (Zhang et al., 2026), and its sensitivity to
the training prompt (Burdisso et al., 2026). What
these share is a fixed decoder.

Decoder specialization. We use the Tiny Aya
family (Salamanca et al., 2026) as the frozen decoder: four instruction-tuned variants of the same
3.35B-parameter multilingual base model, sharing one tokenizer, so that swapping one variant
for another changes only the composition of its
post-training data. Tiny Aya Global is post-trained
across all regions, while each regional variant
reweights the mix toward one cluster of regions:
Earth toward Europe, West Asia and Africa; Fire
toward South Asia; and Water toward Europe, West
Asia and Asia-Pacific. We assign each study language the region of its cluster in the report (Table 1).

Experimental Setup

Datasets and languages. We train on WorldSpeech (Asonitis et al., 2026), a corpus of formal speech from parliamentary proceedings, international broadcasts and public-domain audiobooks, excluding clips of 30 seconds or longer.
The study covers twelve languages (Table 1): four
from the Water region (English, French, Spanish,
Indonesian), four from Fire (Hindi, Marathi, Tamil,
Urdu), three from Earth (Amharic, Hausa, Swahili),
and Kreol Seselwa (Seychellois Creole; hereafter
Seselwa), an African language supported by neither
the encoder nor any Tiny Aya decoder, included to

Training. Every run trains only the projector
with AdamW at learning rate 1.5 × 10−3 , batch
size 8 with gradient accumulation 64 (effective
batch 512), 1,000 steps, no weight decay, on one
NVIDIA H200 GPU, with CER evaluated every
10 steps (101 evaluations per run). The grid is 12
languages × 4 decoders = 48 runs. Amharic and
Seselwa across all four decoders — eight cells —
were trained twice with different seeds, giving replicate pairs from which we measure the run-to-run
noise floor directly.

Voxtral-Mini-3B (Mistral AI, 2025) and Qwen2-

5.1

Regional matching does not change what
the model hears

the ten African languages sum to 0.1% of the Fire
mix (0.010% apiece), and South Asia sums to 0.3%
of Water against 0.1% each for Hindi and Marathi,
leaving 0.017% apiece for the remaining six.
The effect scales with neither characterisation.
Regressing the per-language effect on the log exposure ratio gives Spearman ρ = −0.39 on the
selection curve and −0.04 on held-out in-domain
data; the strongest association anywhere is Pearson
r = −0.51 (p = 0.11). Even though exposure
varies by a factor of roughly 400 across these languages, recognition accuracy does not follow it.
English is the informative exception to the labels.
It is a Water language, but the Fire mix contains
46.2% English against Water’s 17.0%, so English
is the one language whose matched decoder saw
substantially less of it — and English is also the
single language where the matched decoder does
worst (+7.39 CER). Where the region label and
the actual exposure disagree, the outcome follows
the exposure.

Table 2 reports, for each language, the best CER
of the region-matched decoder against the mean of
the two mismatched regional decoders and against
Global. Across the eleven languages covered by a
Tiny Aya region, the matched decoder is 0.55 CER
better on average, with a 95% bootstrap confidence
interval of [−3.89, 1.99]; a Wilcoxon signed-rank
test — a paired significance test over the eleven perlanguage differences — finds no significant difference (p = 0.97). The matched decoder is favoured
by six of eleven languages. Figure 2 shows the
same picture across whole training trajectories.
Two reference numbers say how to read this
null result. The first is the design’s detection limit:
given how much the per-language differences vary,
a paired test at the usual 5% significance level with
80% power would only reliably detect a true advantage of at least 4.90 CER. The second is training
noise: retraining the same configuration with a different seed changes its best CER by 0.89 on average
(the between-run standard deviation over the eight
replicate pairs). The observed 0.55 CER advantage
is below the detection limit, and smaller than the
difference between two identical runs.

5.3

The null replicates on held-out test sets

The contrast above is measured on the curve each
checkpoint was selected against. Evaluating all
four decoders on the held-out test sets repeats it on
data no checkpoint was chosen for. On FLEURS
test the matched decoder is 0.67 CER better on average (p = 0.83); on the in-domain WorldSpeech test
it is 1.71 CER worse (p = 0.70). Urdu’s in-domain
test is the one cell where no decoder transcribes
at all (the best of the four sits at 75.3 CER against
36.6 for the next-worst cell); setting it aside leaves
−0.02 CER over ten languages with a minimum
detectable effect of 0.44. Restricting instead to the
seven languages the manipulation actually treats —
English, Spanish, French and Urdu appear in every
regional mix, with exposure ratios of 0.53–2.72
against 20 or more for the rest — gives −0.09 CER
(p = 0.69) against a minimum detectable effect of
0.67 CER.
Two observations complete the picture. The
sign of the effect is not stable across evaluation
sets (−0.55 selection, −0.67 FLEURS, +1.71 indomain), which is what noise looks like. And the
two largest per-language effects are properties of
the read-speech benchmark rather than of the decoder: Tamil favours its matched decoder by 14.70
CER on the selection curve and 19.58 on FLEURS
test, but by +0.02 on its own in-domain test; English moves from +7.39 to +0.19.

Conclusion

We asked whether pairing a language with its
regionally specialized decoder improves speech
recognition in the SLAM-ASR framework. Across
twelve languages and four Tiny Aya decoders, it
does not. The matched decoder is 0.55 CER better on average (p = 0.97), the result repeats on
held-out test sets, and giving a decoder more targetlanguage data — up to 236 times more — does
not improve its recognition of that language. What
does matter is training the projector at all. On
low-resource languages, the trained projector improves over off-the-shelf models by up to 102.6
CER, including on Seselwa, a language neither
frozen component supports. The practical advice
