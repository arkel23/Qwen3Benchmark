ABSTRACT Post-training quantization (PTQ) is a common route to deploying large automatic speech

recognition (ASR) models, yet its effect on Mandarin ASR is usually reported as a single character error
rate (CER), which hides how a model fails. We evaluated 19 models on 7 Mandarin dataset splits under
3 PTQ methods from 2 to 8 bits, measuring quantization cost in CER and decomposing errors into
linguistically meaningful components: tone and homophone confusions, and consonant and vowel errors
in the base syllable. Quantization robustness depends on model capacity within an architecture family but
does not transfer across families: at 3-bit, median ∆CER within Whisper ranges from +751.8 for Wh-T
to +1.1 for Wh-L, while the 24B Voxtral-S degrades by +34.2 compared with only +1.1 for the 1.5B
Wh-L, and the best BFloat16 model is not the most robust one. CER is strongly correlated with consonant
and vowel errors (ρ = 0.92 for both), more than with tone and homophone errors (ρ = 0.55 and 0.11).
Tone degradation nevertheless remains measurable: among syllables whose base pronunciation is correctly
recovered, tone errors increase consistently with quantization.
INDEX TERMS Automatic speech recognition, error analysis, post-training quantization.

I. Introduction

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is a core technology
behind voice assistants, automatic transcription and speech
translation. Large multilingual models such as Whisper,
Qwen2-Audio, Qwen2.5-Omni and Voxtral [1]–[4] have
raised recognition accuracy across languages and domains,
and their quality is reported as a single error rate per
model. These models range from hundreds of millions to
tens of billions of parameters, and their memory and compute
requirements rule out edge devices and mobile hardware.
Post-training quantization (PTQ) is the usual solution. It
alleviates memory and inference cost without retraining, so
quantized models are available on more hardware. Its cost
for ASR has been measured mostly on English. Recent
PTQ studies benchmark Whisper and Moonshine on English
test sets [5], [6], and the one multilingual study finds that
quantization amplifies the penalty on smaller models and
under-represented languages [7]. All of them report that same

single error rate per model, which says how much a model
fails but not how.
Mandarin Chinese is the second largest language by number of speakers after English, with an estimated 1.2 billion
speakers in 2026 [8]. It is also a major language for digital
communication and voice-based applications, which makes
efficient Mandarin speech recognition relevant to a large and
geographically distributed user base. Mandarin gives a model
more ways to be wrong than English does. A character can be
wrong in its base syllable, through an initial or final error.
It can be wrong in its tone alone, with the base syllable
right. It can be wrong in the character alone, a homophone
with syllable and tone right. The character error rate (CER)
counts all three alike, so a single number cannot separate
them (Figure 1).
Work on Mandarin ASR error correction shows that pronunciation and homophone confusions dominate the errors of
full-precision models [9]–[11]. Compression is known to hit
rare, fine-grained distinctions first, in vision [12] and across

Tone and homophone errors are therefore the natural first
casualties of quantization in Mandarin, and a single error rate
cannot show whether the three failure modes degrade alike.
This leads to a natural question: how does quantization
affect Mandarin speech recognition, and which part of
the syllable does it damage?
We evaluate quantized ASR and large audio-language
models (LALMs) on Mandarin short-form and long-form
speech, and decompose every error through pinyin. We
go one level deeper than prior pinyin-level analyses by
splitting the base syllable into its consonant and vowel. Our
contributions are:
• A study of quantized Mandarin ASR comprising 2,014
evaluation runs: 19 models, 7 quantization configuration, 7 Mandarin short-form splits and a 7-split longform corpus.
• The finding that quantization robustness follows model
capacity within an architecture family but not across
families, and not full-precision accuracy either. At 3bit the median ∆CER within Whisper runs from +751.8
for Wh-T to +1.1 for Wh-L, while the 24B Voxtral-S
degrades by +34.2 against +1.1 for the 1.5B Wh-L.
• A pinyin-level error analysis that decomposes recognition errors into consonant, vowel, tone and homophone components and locates quantization damage
in the base syllable. The Spearman rank correlation ρ
between CER and the consonant and vowel error rates
is 0.92 for both, against 0.55 for the conditional tone
error rate and 0.11 for homophones.
II. Related Work
A. Modern ASR Models

The models we evaluate span three classes. Whisper [1]
is an encoder-decoder trained on large-scale weakly supervised speech-text pairs, which made robust multilingual and
zero-shot recognition standard. Large audio-language models
(LALMs) such as Qwen2-Audio [2], Qwen2.5-Omni [3] and
Voxtral [4] feed an audio encoder into a large language
model and extend speech processing beyond transcription;
their decoders are the same networks whose quantization
the LLM literature studies. Efficiency-oriented variants such
as LiteASR [14], which compresses Whisper’s encoder by
low-rank approximation, push the accuracy-cost frontier and
2

are the natural companions of quantization on constrained
hardware. Section III-A lists the 19 checkpoints.
B. Mandarin ASR and Its Error Structure

Mandarin error analysis works through pinyin. The ASREC benchmark [9] is, to our knowledge, the first dedicated
benchmark for evaluating LLMs on Chinese ASR error
correction, built from the THCHS-30 [15], AISHELL-1 [16],
AISHELL-2 [17] and WenetSpeech [18] transcripts. Pinyinbased representations and regularization model pronunciation and homophone errors better, both for prompting and
fine-tuning correction LLMs [10] and for N-best rescoring
[11], and pinyin has been fed directly into an LLM-based
recognizer [19]. These works agree that Chinese homophones make substitution the dominant Mandarin error, and
they analyze the syllable through its initial and final, the
standard decomposition of Mandarin phonology [20].
They also share a limit. All of them target error correction
or model design and analyze full-precision models; none
asks how the error structure changes when the model’s
precision is cut. To our knowledge no prior work benchmarks
the interaction between PTQ and pinyin-level, let alone
consonant- and vowel-level, Mandarin error structure. That
is the question here.
C. Quantization of Speech Models

Quantization reduces the memory footprint and compute
cost of a network by storing its weights in fewer bits;
Gholami et al. [21] survey the field. We use three PTQ
toolkits: bitsandbytes [22], which implements LLM.int8()
[23] and NF4 [24], Quanto [25], and HQQ [26]. HQQ is
a calibration-free method in the optimization-based line that
GPTQ opened [27], the line that pushes usable bit-widths
below 4. The toolkits and bit-widths evaluated are described
in Section III-C.
For speech, PTQ has been benchmarked on Whisper and
Moonshine in English [5], [6] and combined with token
pruning for ASR hardware [28]; quantization-aware training
has been applied to audio source separation [29]. The one
multilingual study shows that quantizing Whisper amplifies
its penalty on smaller variants and under-represented languages [7]. All of these report an aggregate error rate.
Findings from LLM quantization set the expectations our
results test. Four-bit weight-only PTQ is close to the bit-level
optimum [30] and keeps performance comparable to full
precision [31], and robustness depends on training compute
and dynamics rather than on size alone [32], [33]. Across 1to 4-bit training, ParetoQ reports a sharp transition between
3 and 2 bits, below which the weight representations change
drastically [34]. We will meet the same transition in speech.
The damage is also uneven, which is where the tone
and homophone hypothesis comes from. Compression disproportionately affects long-tail, low-frequency classes in
vision [12] and disparately harms non-Latin-script and lowerresource languages under LLM quantization [13]. In machine

[35], and 4-bit losses grow on long inputs and non-English

None of these studies decomposes the error below the word
or character, and none covers a tonal language. This paper

C. Quantization Methods

We evaluate 6 post-training quantization (PTQ) configurations: bitsandbytes (BNB) [22] at 4-bit (NF4 [24]) and 8-bit
(LLM.int8() [23]), Quanto [25] at 8-bit, and HQQ [26] at
2-, 3-, and 4-bit precision. We denote these configurations
as BNB4, BNB8, Quanto8, HQQ2, HQQ3, and HQQ4,
respectively. HQQ quantizes each weight matrix group-wise;
we use its default group size of 64, with the per-group
scale and zero point kept in bfloat16 and not themselves
quantized. Both the group size and the precision of those
constants matter at 2 bits, so the HQQ2 results below are
specific to this configuration. For the LiteASR models the
integration quantizes the dense matrices only, leaving the
low-rank factors of each compressed layer in bfloat16.
D. Error Metrics

III. Experimental Setup
A. Models

Our experiments use 19 models, shown in Table 1, in four
families: Whisper, LiteASR, Qwen and Voxtral. Whisper
names give the size (T/B/S/M/L) or version (Lv2, Lv3), with
Lv3t the large-v3-turbo variant. LiteASR names carry a t-/lprefix for the teacher checkpoint (large-v3-turbo or large-v3)
and an -f/-a suffix for the fast/accurate variant, so the six
form two compression families rather than one size ladder.
Qwen2 and Qwen2-Ins are Qwen2-Audio-7B and its Instruct
variant; Voxtral-Mi and Voxtral-S are Voxtral-Mini-3B and
Voxtral-Small-24B.

In addition to CER, we evaluate several Chinese-specific
error metrics that decompose recognition errors into
pronunciation-related components. For each utterance, the
reference sequence is the ground-truth transcript, while the
hypothesis sequence is the model output. The two sequences
are aligned on their pinyin representations, with insertions
and deletions always counted as errors.
• Base-syllable error rate (BSER): word error rate on
base-pinyin (tone-stripped) syllable sequences, measuring syllable recognition independent of tone.
• Consonant error rate (CONER) and vowel error rate
(VER): word error rates computed independently on the
consonant (initial, C ) and vowel (final, V ) sequences

8-bit quantization has little effect on CER. Figure 2 shows
only marginal CER changes at 8 bits: the median ∆CER
over the 19 models is 0.0 under Quanto8 and 0.1 under
BNB8. The one model that degrades by more than one point
is Qwen2-Ins under Quanto8 (+6.3).
4-bit quantization costs more than 8-bit but stays manageable. The increase at HQQ4 is visible in Figure 2 yet
small for most of the grid, and the damage is confined to
the smaller models: Wh-T, LiteASR-t and Wh-B degrade by
+20.3, +13.2 and +7.5 ∆CER, and the two Voxtral models
by +2.8 and +3.0. Of the remaining 14 models, 12 change
by less than one point (−0.1 to +0.9) and two, Qwen2Ins and LiteASR-tf, improve by 6.2 and 2.5. Under BNB4
the largest degradation is Qwen2-Ins at +181.6, followed by
Wh-T at +33.7.
3-bit quantization breaks the smaller models. At HQQ3
the smallest Whisper encoders collapse: Wh-T by +751.8
and Wh-B by +129.9 ∆CER (Table 7, Figure 3). The audiolanguage models also degrade sharply, Qwen2 by +106.6,
Voxtral-Mi by +53.1 and Voxtral-S by +34.2, while Wh-L
holds at +1.1 and Qwen2.5-Omni at +1.7.
2-bit quantization fails for most models. At HQQ2 every
model exceeds 20 CER and only 6 of 19 keep a median CER
below 100 (Figure 3). No audio-language model stays below
589 CER on any of the 7 dataset splits. Qwen2.5-Omni, the
best model at BFloat16, collapses to 1575.4. The survivors
are all encoder-decoder ASR models: 4 Whisper-family
models (Wh-L, LiteASR-la, LiteASR-lf and LiteASR-l) stay
below 100 on every split. The cliff between 3 and 2 bits is
the transition ParetoQ reports for LLM weights, which stay
near their pre-trained distribution down to 3 bits and change
drastically below [34]; PTQ has no retraining with which to
absorb that change.
B. Effect of Model Capacity on Quantization Robustness

IV. Results

Larger models are more robust within the same architecture family. Focusing on Whisper, whose checkpoints form
a clean capacity ladder, baseline CER falls with size along
the tiny-to-large ladder, from 44.1 for Wh-T to 7.7 for Wh-L
(Table 4). So does the HQQ3 penalty (Table 7): +751.8 for
Wh-T and +129.9 for Wh-B, flattening from Wh-S upward
at +3.9, +1.1 (Wh-L) and +1.5 (Wh-Lv3). The benefit of
capacity is largest among the small models.

Model weights do not explain quantization robustness.
We next asked whether the weights themselves say which
checkpoint will survive. Quantizing every weight to 2 bits
with one group-wise round-to-nearest scheme, we measured
two properties per checkpoint: the relative reconstruction
error, how far the quantized weights land from the originals,
and the excess kurtosis of the weight distribution, how heavy
its tails are and so how many outliers a fixed grid has to span.
Among the 1.5B Whisper checkpoints both run against the
outcome. Wh-L has the larger reconstruction error (23.0%

2 bits. Within LiteASR the statistics do separate the two

D. Base-Syllable Errors Dominate Quantization
Degradation

Base-syllable errors drive CER degradation. We decompose every error into consonant (CONER), vowel (VER), tone
(TER) and homophone (HER) components to see which part
of Mandarin recognition quantization affects most; Figures 4
and 5 show the decomposition across conditions and across
capacity. CER is rank-correlated with BSER at ρ = 0.98 and
with both VER and CONER at ρ = 0.92; CONER and VER are
almost perfectly correlated with each other (ρ = 0.998), so
quantization does not prefer either component. Part of this
agreement is structural: a consonant or vowel error is also a
base-syllable error and therefore a character error, so one lost
syllable moves all four metrics at once. Tone and homophone
errors track CER far more loosely, at ρ = 0.09 for TER and
ρ = 0.11 for HER, and the figures show the same picture:
CER, CONER and VER rise together as quantization becomes
more aggressive and capacity falls, while TER and HER stay
comparatively flat. Degradation is a failure to recover the
base syllable; why the tone metrics disagree with each other
is explored further in Section IV-E.

E. Tone Errors and the Conditional Denominator

Tone and homophone errors rise with quantization, but
less than base-syllable errors. Across all six conditions the
paired changes correlate with ∆CER at ρ = 0.24 for TER and
0.22 for HER as reported in Table 6. Both rise sharply once
HQQ2 is excluded, to 0.56 and 0.45, and that gap is itself
informative. At 2 bits many models emit repeated or spurious

characters, so CER inflates through insertions against a fixed
reference, while TER and HER are counted only on aligned
pairs and cannot follow. Outside the collapse regime both
track overall degradation, still less closely than the basesyllable metrics do.
Tone errors increase more consistently when the base
syllable is correct. Similar to HER, TER - B is a conditional

read as corpus-wide severity, and an unconditional one can
fall while tone recognition collapses.
Quantization causes a broad loss of tone information.

Its diagonal mean, the share of tones read correctly averaged
over the five tones, holds at 92.2% at BFloat16 and 91.5%
at HQQ4, where the tone error rate rises by 0.7 points,
then reaches 86.6% at 3 bits and falls to 57.9% at 2 bits.

and that tones 2 and 3 are the two lowest at 2 bits, consistent
with previous reports that they are particularly difficult to
distinguish [49]. Every tone loses between 29.2 and 36.8
points from BFloat16 to HQQ2. The off-diagonal mass does
not concentrate: the largest pair carries 11% of the misread
tones at BFloat16 and 11% at HQQ2. Severe quantization
erases tone information across all five tones rather than
sharpening one confusion.
F. Quantization Robustness on Long-Form Audio

HSK-Speech [40] and CMMLU-Speech [41] (35.5–116.8

the long-form corpus, run on the two families that handle
long audio: Voxtral, which chunks internally, and Qwen2.5Omni, with blockwise 2-second attention. CMMLU-Speech
contributes 3,147 utterances against 5–50 per HSK tier, so
the HSK cells of Table 8 are medians over as few as five
utterances and no claim rests on one tier.
Long-form audio preserves the quantization robustness
order. At BFloat16 Qwen2.5-Omni has a median CER at
or below 0.8 on 6 of 7 splits and 18.1 on HSK6, while
Voxtral-S is worse than the smaller Voxtral-Mi on 6 of the 7
(Table 8). Under quantization Qwen2.5-Omni’s median stays
between 0.5 and 0.6 under every condition down to HQQ3,
HQQ4 included, and breaks only at HQQ2, at 223.8; both

V. Conclusion

Across 19 models, 6 PTQ configurations and 2,014 runs,
8-bit quantization costs Mandarin ASR nothing, HQQ4 is
usable for all but the smallest encoders, 3-bit is modeldependent, and 2-bit fails for every audio-language model
and all but four Whisper-family checkpoints. Robustness
follows capacity within an architecture family, not across
families, and not full-precision accuracy: the best BFloat16
model collapses at 2 bits, while the checkpoint that survives
best is the least-trained Whisper large, in line with the
precision scaling law. The damage sits in the base syllable:
CER tracks consonant and vowel errors (ρ = 0.92), and the
two degrade together. Tone degradation is real but hidden by
its denominator: the conventional tone error rate falls under
severe quantization because the syllables it is counted over
disappear, while on the syllables a model still recovers tone
errors climb steadily, across all five tones. Long-form audio
changes none of this.
Limitations. CONER is computed only over non-zero-initial
syllables and is undefined for a few near-total-failure runs.
Conditional tone metrics are unstable under severe collapse,
so severity comparisons use the unconditional TER. Pinyinbased metrics assume Chinese-character output and diverge
from CER when a quantized model emits substantial nonChinese text, the language-confusion failure documented for
text LLMs [52]. The conclusions are specific to the 19
models, PTQ methods and bit-widths evaluated, with at most

Fine-grained image recognition (FGIR) on phones and embedded
devices runs under a fixed compute budget, but the most accurate models are large vision transformers pretrained on ImageNet.
This paper presents teacher-guided data augmentation (TGDA): a
counterfactual attention learning (CAL) teacher’s part attention
module drives per-image cropping and dropping augmentations,
and the same frozen teacher distils into the student. ViTFS is a vision
transformer whose components and normalisation are each chosen
for mobile inference. The student never sees ImageNet: training
on CUB processes 120 million images, 96 percent of which is the
teacher. At 448×448, ViTFS-T with TGDA beats a pretrained DeiT-B
(ImageNet-1k) by 1.9 and 1.6 percentage points on Aircraft and Cars,
and beats a DeiT-T trained from scratch under a prior from-scratch
recipe by 31.9 percentage points, averaged over the three datasets.
Replacing LayerNorm with BatchNorm speeds up on-device inference by 2.64 times at 224 × 224 on the Samsung Galaxy A53 5G,
and is slower than LayerNorm on flagship system-on-chips at that
size. The recipe loses to a counterfactual attention baseline on two

is trained on the target dataset only; the pretrained model is the
teacher, which is not shipped.

2 Related Work
2.1 Fine-grained image recognition
Fine-grained image recognition (FGIR) classifies subordinate categories within a broader class, such as bird species or car models, where inter-class differences are small and intra-class variation is large. Standard benchmarks cover birds, aircraft, and cars
[11, 15, 28]. The dominant recipe pairs a large backbone pretrained
on a generic dataset with a mechanism that localises discriminative parts, whether through learned attention or class activation
maps. Counterfactual attention learning (CAL) [21] trains such an
attention mechanism with counterfactual causality. It compares a
prediction under the learned attention map against a prediction under a counterfactual, non-informative map. Attention is rewarded
for changing that prediction, not merely for correlating with it. This
paper uses a CAL-trained model as its teacher.
A large-scale study of FGIR training and evaluation settings
reports that backbone choice and the training and evaluation protocol both drive the accuracy-cost trade-off. It also reports that
data-aware augmentation during training can recover most of the
accuracy that would otherwise require an inference-time crop [23].
Separately from FGIR, work on convolutional building blocks for
low-resolution images and small objects has established that removing strided convolutions and pooling layers, which discard spatial
detail early, improves accuracy when the input is small (Sunkara
and Luo [25]). This paper’s convolutional stem for low-resolution
inputs follows that same finding rather than superseding it.

• Teacher-guided data augmentation (TGDA): a CAL teacher’s
part attention module (PAM) produces per-image attention
maps that drive attention cropping and attention dropping,
and the same frozen teacher distils into the student through
knowledge distillation (KD).
• ViTFS: a vision transformer whose components – convolutional stem, positional encoding, LayerScale, register tokens
– are each selected against a measurement rather than carried over from ImageNet-scale design. Its normalisation
is chosen for the mobile runtime that executes it, not for
training convenience.
• An evaluation that reports accuracy, inference cost and ondevice latency together, across eleven devices, rather than
accuracy alone, so a gain in one is never read as free in the
others.
• A scope bound the evaluation exposes: TGDA’s gain over
a fine-grained-aware teacher depends on how localisable
object parts are at the student’s resolution, stated plainly
rather than left implicit.

2.2

Knowledge distillation and task-specific
augmentation

Knowledge distillation (KD) trains a smaller student network to
match the output distribution of a larger teacher [9]. FGIR-specific
variants add augmentation driven by the teacher’s own attention:
CEKD [35] cross-distils between augmented views of an ensemble,
and a dynamic semantic-structure distillation method [12] distils
high-resolution teacher structure into a low-resolution student.
Alongside this line, generic augmentation policies transform inputs
without any teacher signal: TrivialAugment [18] samples a single
random operation per image, CutMix [33] and Mixup [34] combine pairs of images and labels, and Random Erasing [37] masks
a random region. Marrie et al. [16] study task-specific distillation
from large pretrained visual models. They report that established
distillation practice needs revisiting for such teachers, and that a
diffusion-based Mixup variant complements standard augmentation
when downstream samples are scarce. Their setting is distillation
from a generically pretrained teacher rather than a fine-grainedspecialised one.

At 448 × 448, ViTFS-T trained with TGDA exceeds the ImageNet1k-pretrained DeiT-B on Aircraft and Cars while training on a far
smaller corpus, and trails the ImageNet-21k-pretrained baselines
on CUB (Table 4). Against other efficient vision transformers it
reaches comparable accuracy at a smaller parameter and FLOP
count (Table 5). Replacing LayerNorm with BatchNorm is faster
at 448 × 448 on most of the eleven measured devices, and slower

2.3

Efficient vision transformers and on-device
inference

A separate line designs vision transformers (ViTs) for inference
cost rather than for accuracy alone. EfficientViT [13] restructures
attention for memory efficiency, LeViT [8] and MobileViT [17]
hybridise convolutional and attention stages for faster inference,
and Swin Transformer [14], PVT [29], NesT [36], and TinyViT [30]
build hierarchical or distilled variants that trade representational
capacity for lower cost. These families optimise throughput or
FLOPs on generic image classification, not FGIR.
ViTFS (Section 4) draws individual components from this literature rather than proposing new ones. A convolutional stem replaces
the linear patch embedding [31]; LayerScale [27] and register tokens [4] stabilise training and remove high-norm artifact tokens;
and BatchNorm [10] replaces LayerNorm [2] in the transformer
blocks. Normalisation choice in transformers has been studied for
reasons beyond accuracy, including runtime behaviour on hardware not designed for LayerNorm’s per-token statistics [24]. What
is missing from the efficient-ViT literature is a cost account that
matches how FGIR is deployed. These families are evaluated at ImageNet scale under large-scale pretraining, and their reported cost is
FLOPs rather than latency measured on the mobile and embedded
devices FGIR models actually run on.

Teacher-Guided Data Augmentation (TGDA)
4

TGDA trains a student from scratch on the target dataset in two
stages, shown in Figure 2. A teacher is trained first and then frozen;
it then supervises the student with KD on the original image and on
augmented views the teacher itself generates. Training the teacher
first, rather than jointly with the student, lets the augmentation
depend on parts the teacher has already learned to localise on the
target dataset, instead of a fixed, hand-designed schedule.
The teacher is a torchvision ResNet-101 pretrained on ImageNet1k and fine-tuned on the target dataset with CAL [21]. CAL trains
a part attention module (PAM) on top of the backbone: a convolutional head that turns an intermediate feature map into a set of
attention maps, one per learned part. TGDA samples one map from
that set per augmentation, weighted toward the maps carrying
more attention mass, so a crop and a drop of the same image can
be guided by different parts.
The sampled map drives two augmentations. Attention cropping
zooms into the region the map highlights; attention dropping masks
that region instead, so the student sees a complementary view of
the same object. Figure 1 shows sampled PAM maps beside the
class activation maps (CAMs) that prior FGIR-KD augmentation
methods use [12, 35]; Figure 3 shows the crops and drops those maps

Vision Transformer From Scratch (ViTFS)

Training a vision transformer (ViT) from scratch on a few thousand
fine-grained images, for inference on a phone, is a different design
problem from adapting a large pretrained ViT. The stock ViT-T/16
row of Table 1 is that problem unaddressed: without pretraining
or any of the changes below, the architecture is the starting point
ViTFS departs from. Every component ViTFS adds is taken from
prior work and evaluated here in this from-scratch, TGDA-trained,
mobile-inference setting rather than proposed as new.
The first group replaces the linear patch embedding with a convolutional stem, adds global average pooling in place of a class
token, and replaces the learned positional embedding with a twodimensional sinusoidal encoding [31]. Removing strided downsampling early in a convolutional stem is reported to help lowresolution inputs specifically [25]; ViTFS’s stem follows that same
finding. Mean over the three datasets, this group alone gains 26.4
pp at 448 × 448, relative to the stock ViT-T/16 baseline. At 224 × 224
the gain is 13.6 pp, and with the teacher at 448 × 448 and the student
at 224 × 224 it is 32.2 pp.
Adding LayerScale [27] and register tokens [4], both randomly
initialised, brings the cumulative mean gain to 28.7 pp at 448 × 448,
12.4 pp at 224 × 224, and 33.7 pp in the mixed-resolution setting.

6.1 Latency and memory on edge devices
Does the normalisation choice that raises ViTFS-T’s accuracy also
help on the hardware it is deployed on? Some mobile inference
runtimes ship an accelerated BatchNorm kernel and no equivalent
for LayerNorm, so a kernel-level advantage would only reach practice if it survives on real devices. On the Samsung Galaxy A53 5G,
the device with the largest measured gain, replacing LayerNorm
with BatchNorm gives a 2.64× latency speedup and a 19% reduction
in peak memory at 224 × 224. At 448 × 448 the same substitution
gives a 1.21× speedup and a 24% reduction (Table 3, Figure 4). The
same substitution is not a uniform win: the speedup sits near parity
on most Pixel devices, and falls below one on flagship system-onchips at 224 × 224 and 672 × 672, where BatchNorm is slower than
LayerNorm. Peak memory follows a related but not identical pattern: BatchNorm reduces it on the Samsung Galaxy A53 5G at both
resolutions, and the reduction is likewise not uniform across the
other ten devices (Table 3). Choosing a normalisation layer for a
deployment target therefore requires measuring that target, not
assuming the kernel-level trend transfers across devices.

Limitations

The training recipe requires a ResNet-101 pretrained on ImageNet1k and then fine-tuned with counterfactual attention learning (CAL);
the deployed student never sees ImageNet, but the pipeline that
produces it does. For a CUB student, the teacher accounts for 96%
of the 120 million images the pipeline processes in total. No experiment in this paper trains the teacher from scratch, so the paper does
not bound how much of the student’s accuracy comes from that
pretraining. The comparison against pretrained baselines is not a
comparison against zero pretraining; it is a comparison against a
smaller, differently sourced pretraining budget that sits upstream
of the student.
With a ResNet-18 student, TGDA is worse than CAL with knowledge distillation (CAL+KD) on SoyLocal and SoyGene (Table 7);
with a ViT-T/16 student it is not. The augmentation assumes discriminative parts that are spatially localisable at the student’s resolution.
That assumption is not met by the ResNet-18 student on these
ultra-fine-grained leaf images, whose classes separate by leaf shape
and vein pattern rather than by object parts. The paper does not
establish which of resolution, architecture or dataset granularity is
responsible for the loss.
Replacing LayerNorm with BatchNorm is not a uniform latency
win (Table 3). The LayerNorm/BatchNorm latency ratio falls below
one on flagship system-on-chips and sits near parity on most Pixel
devices, so the normalisation choice is a per-target decision, not a
default. Each cell in that table is a single measurement on one build
of the model.
Every accuracy cell in the paper is a single run. The paper reports
no variance, so small differences between rows are not distinguishable from run-to-run noise, and an ordering between two settings
that differ by a small margin is not established at this scale.
The distillation constants 𝛽 and 𝑇 were chosen on CUB, and the
sweeps that examine them (Table 9, Table 10) cover CUB with a
ResNet-18 student only. The paper does not show that the same constants are optimal for the other datasets or for the ViTFS students.
