in Multilingual ASR Quantization

Abstract
Multilingual Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) accuracy is correlated to how
much pretraining data a language had. A previous study reports that quantization
further compounds the gap between high- and low-resource languages. We show
that this conclusion is a property of how degradation was measured, an absolute
threshold on the error increase and we ask what governs quantization fragility
instead. On 18 ASR and audio-language models, we evaluate the effect of posttraining quantization (HQQ at 8, 6, 4, and 3 bits) against the BFloat16 baseline
across 25 European languages. We find that the amount of pretraining data has
little explanatory power for the quantization penalty, while model capacity is
significantly more important. Across the Whisper scaling ladder, the relative
CER penalty at 4 bits falls from 129.3% for Whisper-T to 2.8% for Whisper-Lv3.
This capacity effect becomes stronger as the bit-width decreases. Overall, model
capacity has a greater impact on quantization robustness than the choice of HQQ
bit-width, particularly for the larger models.

Introduction

Whisper-style models [Radford et al., 2023] and audio-language models [Chu et al., 2024, Xu
et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025] enable ASR for dozens of languages, but quality is highly uneven.
High-resource languages reach single-digit error rates while low-resource languages lag far behind.
Deployment introduces another concern through quantization, raising the possibility that the two
inequalities compound. That concern is well studied in text: 4-bit machine translation and quantized
multilingual LLMs degrade sharply for low-resource and non-Latin-script languages [Marchisio et al.,
2024, Marie and Fujita, 2025]. For speech, Ferraz [2024] reports the same pattern, with low-resource
languages appearing to be the most adversely affected by quantization.
We find that this apparent disparity is a property of the measurement rather than of quantization.
Ferraz [2024] classifies an utterance as degraded when its error rate rises by more than five points,
measured with LLM.int8() [Dettmers et al., 2022] at 8-bit. A fixed absolute threshold flags the
languages with the highest baselines, regardless of what quantization does to them. And baselines
here differ by an order of magnitude depending on the hours of pretraining data a language received,
which we called resourcing. Separating the absolute penalty from the relative one dissolves the effect.
The absolute penalty grows as resourcing falls in proportion to the baseline, while the relative penalty
varies by much less. CER also falls log-linearly with pretraining hours, with Pearson correlations of
-0.60 to -0.73 across the leading models, and every family’s median CER is at least three times higher
in the low tier than in the high tier (Appendix C). This raises a natural question: does quantization
further widen this existing gap?
Our design makes that separation trustworthy. Every quantized evaluation is paired with its own
BFloat16 baseline on the same (model, dataset), so each delta compares one checkpoint against itself

at two precisions. The grid covers 18 models, 25 languages and four bit-widths of Half-Quadratic
Quantization (HQQ) [Badri and Shaji, 2023], a calibration-free post-training method that applies
uniformly across languages. When we ask what predicts the penalty, model capacity within a fixed
architecture emerges as the dominant factor, with compressed checkpoints more fragile than their
size suggests.

50

Two recent studies quantize small ASR models. Edge-ASR [Feng et al., 2025] benchmarks eight
PTQ methods on Whisper and Moonshine checkpoints up to 244M parameters, and Söhler et al.
[2026] compare quantization libraries on Whisper tiny, base and small. Both find that the larger of
these small models withstand low bit-widths better, and both evaluate only English. We extend this
line in two directions: to billion-scale models, and across 25 languages whose resourcing spans three
orders of magnitude. Our contributions are threefold. We separate the absolute from the relative
quantization penalty and show the apparent resourcing effect belongs to the former. We show that
model capacity, not resourcing, is what decides how much a model loses. And we show a compressed
checkpoint breaking the size trend, losing far more than plain models of its size.

Experimental Setup

Models. 18 ASR and audio-language models spanning four families and three orders of magnitude:
8 Whisper checkpoints [Radford et al., 2023] (including the compressed turbo variant Wh-Lv3t),
6 LiteASR compressions of Whisper-large-v3 [Kamahori et al., 2025], 2 Qwen audio-LLMs [Chu
et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025] and 2 Voxtral models [Liu et al., 2025], from 37.8M to 24B parameters.
Datasets. 25 European languages of short-form speech (utterances under 30s) from Common
Voice 15 [Ardila et al., 2020], MLS [Pratap et al., 2020] and FLEURS [Conneau et al., 2022], giving
47 evaluation sets (Appendix A). Each language is grouped by its combined Whisper pretraining
hours into four resource tiers: low (<316 h), medium (316–3,162 h), high (3,162–31,623 h) and
extra (≥31,623 h, English only). Table 1 lists each tier’s hour range and its number of evaluation
sets. We report character error rate (CER), which tracks human judgement more closely than WER
for multilingual ASR [K et al., 2025]; scoring with WER changes no conclusion (Appendix D).
Quantization. We use HQQ [Badri and Shaji, 2023] at 8-, 6-, 4- and 3-bit weight-only quantization
with group size 64. All unquantized components use BFloat16, which serves as our BFloat16 baseline.
Quantization covers every linear layer except the output head; the convolutional layers, LayerNorm,
and embeddings remain in BFloat16. Every bit-width covers the same roster, and every run uses
offline batch decoding with BFloat16 activations and greedy decoding. The decoder’s language token
is forced per evaluation set to prevent language identification from affecting the measured deltas.
Since HQQ requires no calibration data, the quantization procedure is applied uniformly across
languages. A run whose CER reaches 200 is treated as failed rather than degraded and is excluded;
results are reported as medians (Appendix B).

76

Statistical analysis. We use the Kruskal–Wallis test [Kruskal and Wallis, 1952] to compare relative
CER penalties across multiple resource tiers or models, and the Mann–Whitney test [Mann and
Whitney, 1947] for direct comparisons between two groups. Both are nonparametric tests based on
the ranks of the observations, avoiding assumptions about normally distributed penalties. We use
p < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance.

Across the pooled runs, we find little evidence that resourcing explains the relative quantization

Applying the same analysis across models shows a much larger difference than across resource tiers.
At 4 bits, the relative CER penalty ranges from 129.3% for Wh-T to 2.8% for Wh-Lv3. The penalty
falls consistently as model size increases, with the largest reductions occurring at the small end of the
Whisper scaling ladder. This pattern is much stronger than the small differences observed between
resource tiers.

Model capacity strongly affects quantization fragility

Model capacity, measured by parameter count, is the main source of variation in quantization fragility,
but its effect depends on the architecture. Across all 16 models, parameter count has only a weak
correlation with relative penalty (Spearman ρ=−0.28). For example, Voxtral-Mini (4,676M) has a
larger relative penalty of 18.9% from BFloat16 to 4-bit quant than the smaller LiteASR-la (1,338M),
3

whose penalty is only 2.4%. Thus, parameter count alone is not a reliable predictor across
different architectures.
However, within a single architecture the relationship is much clearer. Restricted to the Whisper
models, which form a consistent scaling ladder, relative penalty decreases strongly with parameter
count (Spearman ρ=−0.88), as shown in Figure 1 and Table 2. The penalty falls monotonically
from Wh-T through Wh-L, then flattens across the three 1.5B checkpoints, showing that the benefit
of additional capacity is largest at the smaller end of the ladder and becomes smaller at the
top. Voxtral’s two checkpoints show the same trend (18.9% at 4.7B against 10.8% at 24.3B), while
LiteASR shows why architecture must be held fixed, its variants differ by compression strategy rather
than size, and size barely predicts fragility within it (ρ=−0.18).
The compressed Wh-Lv3t is an important exception. With 809M parameters, it is similar in size to
Wh-M and Wh-L, but its 8.5% relative penalty is 1.9–7.6× larger than that of the plain models of
comparable size. This shows that model size alone does not determine quantization fragility, as the
way a model was trained or compressed can also matter.
The capacity effect becomes stronger as precision falls. The correlation across the Whisper ladder
changes from ρ=−0.15 at 8-bit to -0.31 at 6-bit, -0.70 at 4-bit, and -0.76 at 3-bit. At 8-bit, quantization
adds little to no error, whereas at 3-bit the penalty is strongly associated with model capacity. In
other words, lower bit-widths make capacity increasingly important for quantization robustness,
while resourcing has only a small effect (Sec. 3.1).

135

There are a few caveats to this trend. LiteASR-tf has a very high BFloat16 baseline, making its
0.1% relative penalty look large despite a small absolute increase. The smallest Whisper models also
quantize a smaller fraction of their parameters because the tied embedding and output head remain in
BFloat16, yet they still have the largest penalties. This means the capacity trend is not simply a
result of quantizing more parameters in smaller models. Finally, more 3-bit runs are removed
by the degenerate-run filter than at BFloat16, so the 3-bit results should be interpreted with some
caution (Appendix B).

4

Conclusion

Pretraining hours predict BFloat16 CER well, but have only a limited effect on the relative quantization
penalty. At 4 bits, the low-resource tier has a relative penalty of 6.4% compared with 5.7% for the
high tier, while differences between models are much larger. Within the Whisper scaling ladder, the
4-bit penalty falls from 129.3% for Wh-T to 2.8% for Wh-Lv3, with a strong correlation between
model size and penalty (ρ=−0.88). This relationship becomes stronger as precision is reduced,
showing that model capacity becomes increasingly important for quantization robustness at lower
bit-widths. Overall, our results suggest that quantization does not inherently widen the low-resource
gap; the apparent resource effect is largely explained by differences in BFloat16 baseline error, while
model capacity has a much larger influence on quantization fragility.
For deployment, these results favor the largest plain checkpoint that fits within a fixed architecture,
particularly when using lower bit-widths. The compressed Wh-Lv3t is an important exception,
showing substantially higher fragility than plain models of similar size and indicating that model
history also matters beyond parameter count.

157

Our study uses only one PTQ method, HQQ, and focuses on European short-form speech. The
3-bit results are also affected by the removal of degenerate runs. The capacity trend is based mainly
on the Whisper scaling ladder, with only two Voxtral models and one compressed checkpoint as
additional evidence; the LiteASR family varies in compression strategy rather than size, showing that
differences in training and compression can also affect quantization fragility beyond parameter count.
Further evaluation across quantization methods, architectures, languages, and training strategies is
therefore needed to determine how broadly these findings generalize. Appendix F discusses these
limitations in more detail.

Degenerate runs. A run whose CER reaches 200 has failed rather than degraded, so we drop it
and report medians, which are robust to the remaining heavy tail. The filter removes 0.8% of runs
at BFloat16 but 8.2% at 3-bit. Of those 69 collapses, 68 come from the two smallest Whisper

with WER instead changes no conclusion, the run-level agreement between the two is Spearman

Extended limitations and ethical considerations

Extending to other PTQ families, such as bnb with LLM.int8() and NF4/QLoRA [bitsandbytes
foundation, 2022, Dettmers et al., 2022, 2023], GPTQ [Frantar et al., 2023], Quanto [Hugging Face,
2024], and to non-European languages is future work. Per-tier sample sizes are uneven. We measure
accuracy (CER) only; latency, memory and energy on device are not evaluated. Feng et al. [2025]
and Söhler et al. [2026] also find int8 quantization near-lossless, matching our 8-bit and 6-bit rows;
neither study includes models above 244M parameters, where we find the penalty stops shrinking.
Resource bins follow Whisper’s approximate hour estimates.
All datasets are publicly released benchmarks under research-use licenses; we introduce no new
speech data or speaker-identifying information. The analysis is intended to support more equitable
deployment of quantized multilingual ASR.
