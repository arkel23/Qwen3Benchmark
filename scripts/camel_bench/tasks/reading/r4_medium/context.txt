Cultivar Identification from Leaf Images with a
Semantic Adaptation Warmup for Frozen Vision Transformers
Edwin Arkel Rios Student Member, IEEE, Femiloye Oyerinde, Fernando Mikael, Oswin Gosal, Min-Chun
Hu, Senior Member, IEEE, and Bo-Cheng Lai

Abstract—Identifying the cultivar of a crop from its leaves
underpins breeding programs and seed certification, but it is
done by hand: the visual differences between cultivars are
finer than those between species, and labeled data is scarce.
The usual remedy, adapting a network pretrained on a generic
corpus, transfers poorly: across thirteen configurations built on
a frozen Vision Transformer, accuracy falls from between 82.9%
and 88.7% on a species-level benchmark to at most 69.7% on
the easiest of ten cultivar datasets, and a linear classifier on
the frozen representation reaches only 17.9% on SoyGlobal.
We attribute this to a semantic gap between what generic
pretraining encodes and what separating cultivars requires, and
we close it with a Semantic Adaptation Warmup (SAW): the
inserted adaptation modules are first trained against a supervised
contrastive objective on a pooled corpus of cultivar imagery, and
only then is the classifier trained. The warmup is driven by
a Dual-Attention-driven Mix Augmentation SupCon (DAMAS)
strategy, which locates discriminative regions from the attention
of two early transformer layers rather than a full rollout,
reducing the augmentation cost from the O(L · N 2 ) of a [CLS]row rollout to O(N 2 ). Warmup happens once and adds nothing
to inference cost. Across ten cultivar datasets at 448 × 448 the
warmup raises average top-1 accuracy by 5.9 points over the
same architecture without it, exceeds the strongest competing
parameter-efficient method by 12.6 points and the strongest fully
fine-tuned method in our comparison by 5.1 points, while training
0.51 M parameters per 200-class task, at lower FLOPs and higher
throughput than the alternatives.
Index Terms—Cultivar identification, plant phenotyping, precision agriculture, ultra-fine-grained image recognition, parameterefficient transfer learning, vision transformer, contrastive learning

ELLING one cultivar of a crop from another by eye is
a routine and consequential task in agriculture. Breeding
programmes must track which variety is growing in which
plot, and seed certification depends on confirming varietal
identity; both are done today by trained inspectors working
from leaf morphology. It is slow, and it does not scale: expert
accuracy falls as the number of candidate cultivars grows, and
annotating images to train a model requires the same scarce
expertise, so labeled data stays thin — often only a handful
of images per cultivar. Figure 1 shows what makes the task
hard: leaves of different cultivars look nearly identical, while
leaves of one cultivar vary widely.

Fig. 1. Samples of different classes from the ultra-fine-grained SoyGene
and SoyLocal datasets [2], highlighting subtle inter-class differences and
pronounced intra-class diversity.

Automated visual recognition in agrifood has advanced
quickly on tasks of this shape. Deep networks now classify
potato breeds from tuber and leaf imagery [3], grade defects
in soybean kernels [4], identify individual cattle from muzzle
patterns [5], detect plant disease in real time from a drone [6],
and discriminate visually similar seedlings under varying field
conditions [7]. What these share with cultivar identification is
that the distinction to be made is finer than the species level,
so the discriminative evidence is subtle, distributed, and easily
swamped by variation within a class. This regime is known
as ultra-fine-grained image recognition (UFGIR) [2], and it
separates cultivar identification from conventional fine-grained
recognition, which stops at species-level granularity [8], [9].

A. The problem: pretrained features do not transfer to cultivar
granularity
The standard recipe for a task with few labels is to take
a network pretrained on a large generic corpus and adapt it.
For cultivar recognition that recipe fails at the first step, and
it fails by a wide margin.
We measured this directly. Holding a Vision Transformer’s
[10] pretrained weights frozen and training only lightweight
modules on top, we evaluated thirteen such configurations —
those of Tab. II that were run on both benchmarks — at

The shortfall is visible in the representation itself, before
any adaptation is attempted. We computed the layer-wise
Centered Kernel Alignment [11] of the pretrained encoder on
all eleven datasets under one protocol, with no training of
any kind. Averaged over the deepest third of the encoder,
similarity between layers is 0.56 to 0.57 on the species
benchmark but between 0.709 and 0.743 on every one of the
ten cultivar datasets, at both input resolutions we evaluate: the
two ranges never overlap, on any dataset, at either resolution.
On cultivar imagery the deep layers of the pretrained encoder
have largely stopped distinguishing one layer’s output from
another’s, which is the representational form of the same
failure the accuracies report. A second, independent measure
agrees. At matched class count, the log maximum evidence
[12] of the frozen features is 1.50 on the species benchmark
against 1.23 to 1.26 on every cultivar dataset of comparable
granularity.
This is not a shortage of data. SoyGene carries more training
images than the species benchmark and still scores far lower.
It is a mismatch between what generic pretraining encodes and
what separating cultivars requires — a semantic gap between
the pretraining domain and the target task. Closing it, rather
than merely adding capacity on top of it, is what this article
addresses.
The gap also explains why parameter-efficient adaptation
is the natural setting here rather than an afterthought. Fully
fine-tuning a transformer for every cultivar task is expensive to store and to serve, and deployment in this domain
means a handheld or airborne device rather than a datacenter.
Parameter-efficient transfer learning (PETL) methods [13]–
[15] answer that by freezing the backbone and training small
inserted modules, so one copy of the backbone serves many
tasks. But a frozen backbone is exactly the case where the gap
bites hardest, because the representation cannot move to meet
the task. Two symptoms follow. The deeper encoder layers
converge toward nearly identical representations, a degradation
of representational diversity known as attention collapse [16]–
[19] and visible in the layer-wise similarity of Fig. 4. And
the attention that remains settles on background rather than
on the leaf, as Fig. 2 shows — a shortcut [20] that a model
with few labeled images is readily driven to. A method that
adapts a frozen backbone for cultivar recognition therefore has
to address the gap explicitly.

B. Approach
We introduce a Semantic Adaptation Warmup (SAW): a
stage that trains the inserted adaptation modules against a
supervised contrastive objective, on a pooled corpus of cultivar
imagery, before the classifier is trained. Its augmentation is
data-aware — discriminative regions are located from the
attention of two early transformer layers and swapped between
images, which costs O(N 2 ) rather than the O(L · N 2 ) of
a full attention rollout [22]–[24], where L is the number
of transformer layers and N the sequence length. We call
this augmentation Dual-Attention-driven Mix Augmentation
SupCon (DAMAS). Earlier work has added self-supervised or
contrastive losses to fine-tuning [25]–[28], but trains them on
the target dataset alone and augments without regard to where
the discriminative evidence lies. The warmup adds nothing
to inference cost: it changes only the state the modules start
from. Nor is it tied to the module it initializes: applied to a
plain adapter, with no inter-layer module at all, it is worth 8.8
points across the ten datasets.
Across ten cultivar datasets, the warmup raises average
top-1 accuracy by 5.9 points at 448 × 448 over the same
architecture trained without it, and by 6.7 points at 224 × 224;
against the strongest competing parameter-efficient method,
FFVT, the margins are 12.6 and 11.1 points respectively. It
trains 0.51 M parameters per task, of which the 200-class head
is 0.15 M — the head scales with the label set. Inference costs
67.1 GFLOPs against 78.5 for the frozen baseline, with higher
batched throughput than every parameter-efficient method we
compare against.

On the other side, self-supervised learning has also become
crucial to learn discriminative features with limited data [25]–
[27]. Foundational contrastive methods like SimCLR [34] and
SupCon [35] define positive pairs via data augmentations or
class labels, but their use of generic augmentations limits
performance in fine-grained tasks. Recent extensions, such as
PID [36] and SelfCon [37], introduce stronger cross-image
augmentations [38] or auxiliary modules that increase training
complexity or overlook subtle details critical for UFGIR.
Therefore, current SOTA methods such as MixViT [25],
CLE-ViT [26] and CSDNet [27] have incorporated selfsupervised learning to further enhance the extraction of ultrafine discriminative features; most recently, GAEor [39] sets
the fully fine-tuned state of the art on these benchmarks by
encoding geometric attributes of amplified details as a selfsupervised signal on a Swin-B backbone. However, they employ generic or weak data augmentation which limits feature
learning with scarce data. Additionally, most of these methods
employ transformer-based backbones with a large number of
parameters that all need to be stored for deployment and
require significant resources during training.
To address these limitations our method uses ViT attention
to identify and swap discriminative regions in image pairs
for label-aware contrastive learning. This approach enhances
representation learning under limited data constraints through
the usage of cross-image data-aware augmented positives.
Furthermore, we employ parameter-efficient transfer learning
to reduce number of deployable parameters and training costs,
ensuring our method remains scalable and deployment-friendly
while preserving ultra-fine-grained recognition performance.

B. Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning

Ultra fine-grained image recognition (UFGIR) remains a
challenging task due to subtle inter-class differences and
limited labeled data. To address this, many methods build
on generic recognition backbones and augment them with
modules or loss functions that effectively select and aggregate discriminative features [29]–[32]. For instance, FFVT
[30] leverages ViT attention scores to extract and aggregate
intermediate low-, medium-, and high-level features through
the last transformer encoder block, enhancing sensitivity to
subtle fine-grained differences.

Parameter-efficient transfer learning (PETL) techniques seek
to fine-tune only a small subset of a model’s parameters while
keeping the majority of the pretrained backbone fixed. These
methods generally fall into two categories: prompt-tuning and
adapter-based approaches.

Given the data constraints in UFGIR, tailored data augmentation strategies [2], [31]–[33] are critical. Earlier methods such as SPARE [32] employ CNNs to extract semantic
regions for augmentation. More recent methods make use
of transformer aggregated attention for data-aware augmentations [23], [24], but incur high computational costs since
they employ attention rollout for aggregation [22] which has
complexity of O(L · N 3 ) with respect to the number of
transformer layers L and the sequence length N . In contrast,
our method uses an efficient implementation based on two
early ViT layers, drastically reducing complexity to O(N 2 ).

Prompt-tuning [13] augments the input sequence with additional task-specific, learnable tokens appended at various
stages of the transformer. For instance, VQT [40] employs
these tokens as queries to aggregate layer-wise information,
which is then integrated into the classification head. However,
the incorporation of extra tokens not only increases the computational cost during the forward pass but also leads to a
rapid increase in the parameters for the classification head.
In contrast, adapter-based methods insert lightweight, nonlinear modules directly within transformer layers. Originally
proposed by Houlsby et al. [21] in the natural language
processing domain, adapters have been successfully adapted
for vision transformers. For example, ConvPass [41] and ProTuning [14] extend this concept by integrating 2D convolutions to inject spatial biases, thereby enhancing the extraction

The Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder processes an input
image X ∈ RIS×IS×C , where IS denotes the image side
length and C the number of channels. The image is patchified
using a convolution with kernel size and stride P × P ,
producing SZ1 = IS/P patches along each spatial dimension.
These patches are flattened and linearly projected into Ddimensional embeddings, forming an input token sequence of
length N1 = SZ21 . A learnable classification token ([CLS])
is prepended to the sequence, and positional embeddings are
added to preserve spatial information. The resulting sequence
is then passed through a series of L transformer encoder
blocks, each consisting of multi-head self-attention (MHSA)
and position-wise feed-forward networks (PWFFN) [42]; the
output of each encoder block is denoted as Zl . Finally, a
LayerNorm [43] followed by a linear classifier is applied to
the [CLS] token for prediction.

B. Attention Collapse in Vision Transformers
Recent studies have identified a phenomenon known as
attention collapse in Vision Transformers (ViTs) [17], [19],
where attention weights between tokens become increasingly
uniform across layers. This over-smoothing [44] leads to loss
of high-frequency information and a reduction in representational diversity. Such uniformity is particularly detrimental in

IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AGRIFOOD ELECTRONICS

ultra-fine-grained image recognition (UFGIR), where nuanced
inter-class variations must be preserved and emphasized.
The problem is further exacerbated in parameter-efficient
transfer learning (PETL) settings, where only a small number
of additional parameters (e.g., adapters) are fine-tuned while
the pretrained ViT backbone remains frozen. In such settings,
we observe that the attention matrices of deeper transformer
layers become extremely similar, as measured by the Centered
Kernel Alignment (CKA) similarity shown in Fig. 4. This
indicates redundancy in the feature encoding process, limiting
the ability of the model to extract discriminative features
necessary for fine-grained tasks and highlighting the need
for specialized mechanisms that preserve or enhance attention
diversity during adaptation.

C. ILA: Inter-Layer Adapter
To mitigate attention collapse and encourage the model
to attend to distinct spatial regions throughout the encoding
process, we introduce the Inter-Layer Adapter (ILA) module.
ILA explicitly imposes a hierarchical structure by progressively reducing the spatial resolution of token embeddings
between groups of transformer encoder blocks. This design
draws inspiration from prior work that embeds hierarchical
inductive biases into the model architecture for richer feature
encoding [45], [46].

D. Semantic Adaptation Warmup (SAW) with DAMAS
To further address the challenges brought by data scarcity
in UFGIR, observed in Fig. 2 as overfitting to background
regions, we propose a Semantic Adaptation Warmup (SAW)
stage that allows our ViT with ILA modules to bridge the
semantic gap [28] between generic pretraining and ultra-finegrained recognition.
To address this issue we propose a novel strategy composed
of the three following components:
1) Data Diversity: we pool all ultra-fine-grained datasets to
create a rich, diverse corpus for the adaptation warmup.

2) Supervised Contrastive Warmup: we pretrain the
inter-layer adapters using a SupCon loss to align their

3) Early Attention Data-Aware Augmentation: Use
dual-attention rollout from two early layers to identify
and swap discriminative regions between image
pairs from the same label, generating diverse, labelaware, cross-image augmentations as positives for the
supervised contrastive loss.
After warmup, adapters are fine-tuned on the target dataset.
As shown in Fig. 4, incorporating this warmup stage results
in deeper transformer layers that capture more distinct and
discriminative features, in contrast to the collapsed representations observed with the frozen backbone alone. Figure 5
shows the same comparison on all ten datasets: averaged over
the deepest third of the encoder, similarity falls from 0.727
for the frozen encoder to 0.661 with adapters and 0.634 with
ILA, and the warmed state is lower still at 0.547.

details necessary for UFGIR. To generate label-aware positive
samples, we cut and swap discriminative regions, using the
dual-attention rollout, between image pairs from the same class
as seen in Fig. 6. To obtain the embedding r for the SupCon
loss, we first apply global average pooling to the feature ZL
produced by the final transformer block, and then pass the
resulting vector through a MLP projection head.

attention rollout over L layers is computationally expensive —
O(L · N 3 ) with full matrix products, and still O(L · N 2 ) when
only the [CLS] row is propagated — where N is the sequence
length and L is the number of aggregated layers, while relying
on a single layer often produces noisy or inconsistent maps
[51].
To address this, we propose a dual-attention strategy that
combines attention from two early layers, such as layers 2-3
or 3–4, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Specifically, we extract the
first row of the attention matrix (corresponding to the [CLS]
token) from one layer and multiply it with that of the next
layer, reducing the computation to O(N 2 ) — a factor of L
below a [CLS]-row rollout over all layers, and a factor of
L · N below the full-matrix form. The saving matters because
N grows quadratically with image resolution.
The third and fourth rows of Fig. 7 show that our dualattention maps capture discriminative areas comparable to the
full rollout (second row) while being much more efficient.
These discriminative regions guide our data-aware augmentation by swapping regions between image pairs (see Fig. 6),
enhancing fine-grained feature learning and data diversity for
UFGIR tasks.

In addition, we assess computational efficiency by measuring 1) task-specific trainable parameters (TSP, the number of
parameters fine-tuned for the task), 2) inference floating-point

All models are optimized using Stochastic Gradient Descent

transformer layers, and a hidden dimension D of 768. Reduced

• SAW: extends ILA++ by integrating a semantic adaptation warmup stage, wherein our proposed Dual-Attentiondriven Mix Augmentation SupCon (DAMAS) strategy
pretrains the adapters on a diverse corpus of ultra-finegrained data before fine-tuning. The pooled corpus is the
union of the ten datasets’ training splits — 24,367 images

E. Ablations
1) Design of Residual Spatial Downsampling (RSDS)
Branch: We perform ablation experiments to evaluate the
importance of the Residual Spatial Downsampling (RSDS)
branch in our ILA++ framework. We observe that completely
removing the RSDS branch results in a collapsed representation, with accuracy dropping to near-zero levels, despite
the presence of both adapters and ILA. This confirms that

IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AGRIFOOD ELECTRONICS

13

the RSDS branch residual signal propagation is essential for
enabling the overall architecture to learn.
Second, we examine the impact of removing spatial downsampling from the RSDS path by padding both the main and
residual branches to match dimensions. This configuration fails
to improve upon the baseline with adapters, highlighting the
need for inductive biases in the form of hierarchical feature
extraction.
Finally, we assess various design choices for the RSDS
operator. All alternatives fail to improve upon the adapter
baseline. In particular, depth-wise convolution with Xavier
initialization performs poorly, showing the importance of
initialization for proper residual propagation. In contrast, our
default design incorporating depth-wise convolution initialized
to near one values consistently outperforms all other variants, surpassing the adapter baseline by 8.5 and 8.0 points,
respectively. This confirms that a learnable, identity-preserving
initialization is essential for smoothly bridging the spatial mismatch introduced by downsampling while preserving feature
integrity.
These results validate the design choices made in the RSDS
as a necessary component within ILA, enabling the inclusion
of hierarchical biases for feature extraction in UFGIR.

5) The Warmup Without ILA: Every warmup run reported
so far is built on ILA, so the warmup could be read as a
property of that module rather than a method in its own right.
Table XV settles this by applying it to a plain adapter with
no inter-layer module anywhere in the network. Average top1 accuracy rises from 53.42% to 62.20%, a gain of 8.78
points that holds on all ten datasets and ranges from 3.46 on
SoyAgeing to 15.34 on SoyLocal. The two arms are trained
identically at a fixed learning rate over three seeds, and differ
only in whether the warmup checkpoint is loaded.
The gain is larger than the 6.7 points the warmup adds on
top of ILA at the same 224 × 224 resolution, so the two are
complementary rather than one depending on the other. This is
what the framing in Sec. V-A predicts: the warmup addresses
a mismatch between the pretrained representation and cultivar
recognition, which is a property of the backbone and not of
the module placed on top of it.

data augmentation [38], [65] where attentive regions of two

VI. C ONCLUSION
This work presents a novel framework for parameterefficient ultra-fine-grained image recognition (UFGIR) that
combines a downsampling Inter-Layer Adapter (ILA) module
with Semantic Adaptation Warmup (SAW) stage. The ILA
module introduces inductive bias in the form of hierarchical
feature extraction for frozen Vision Transformers, significantly
reducing deployment parameters and inference cost. To address the semantic gap between generic pretraining and finegrained tasks we proposed SAW, an initialization strategy
that employs a novel Dual-Attention-driven Mix Augmentation
SupCon (DAMAS) objective to align adapter features with the
frozen backbone in a data-efficient manner.
The proposed framework incorporating ILA and SAW
achieves significant accuracy gains across ten UFGIR benchmarks while reducing inference cost and parameter overhead
compared to existing methods. These results highlight the
effectiveness of combining inductive biases with task-specific
pretraining for parameter-efficient adaptation in scenarios with
limited data.

Cross-Layer Cache Aggregation for Token
Reduction in Ultra-Fine-Grained Image Recognition
Edwin Arkel Rios† , Jansen Christopher Yuanda‡ , Vincent Leon Ghanz‡ , Cheng-Wei Yu† , Bo-Cheng Lai† , Min-Chun Hu‡

National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, Taiwan, ‡ National Tsing Hua University, Taiwan

Abstract—Ultra-fine-grained image recognition (UFGIR) is a
challenging task that involves classifying images within a macrocategory. While traditional FGIR deals with classifying different
species, UFGIR goes beyond by classifying sub-categories within
a species such as cultivars of a plant. In recent times the usage
of Vision Transformer-based backbones has allowed methods to
obtain outstanding recognition performances in this task but this
comes at a significant cost in terms of computation specially
since this task significantly benefits from incorporating higher
resolution images. Therefore, techniques such as token reduction
have emerged to reduce the computational cost. However, dropping tokens leads to loss of essential information for fine-grained
categories, specially as the token keep rate is reduced. Therefore,
to counteract the loss of information brought by the usage of
token reduction we propose a novel Cross-Layer Aggregation
Classification Head and a Cross-Layer Cache mechanism to
recover and access information from previous layers in later
locations. Extensive experiments covering more than 2000 runs
across diverse settings including 5 datasets, 9 backbones, 7 token
reduction methods, 5 keep rates, and 2 image sizes demonstrate
the effectiveness of the proposed plug-and-play modules and
allow us to push the boundaries of accuracy vs cost for UFGIR
by reducing the kept tokens to extremely low ratios of up to
10% while maintaining a competitive accuracy to state-of-the-art
models. Code is available at: https://github.com/arkel23/CLCA
Index Terms—Efficient, Vision Transformer, Fine Grained
Visual Analysis, Model Compression, Image Classification.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Fine-grained image recognition (FGIR) involves classifying
images into sub-categories within a larger macro-category [1].
While conventional FGIR [1] classifies objects usually up to
species-level granularity, ultra-FGIR (UFGIR) may categorize
classes at a finer level, such as cultivars of a plant. It has
practical applications in fields such as agriculture [2].
It is challenging due to small inter-class and large intraclass variations, but in recent years there has been a significant
improvement in performance in terms of accuracy. Previous
work has noted how this may be driven by backbone choices
[3]. In particular, the Vision Transformer (ViT) [4] has brought
a large performance improvement across tasks [5], [6]; the
global receptive field of the ViT’s attention mechanism provides it an advantage in handling long-range dependencies,
allowing it to effectively discover and learn dependencies
between discriminative features within an image [3].
This work was supported by the National Science and Technology Council,
Taiwan, under grant NSTC 111-2221-E-A49-092-MY3, NSTC 113-2640-EA49-005, NSTC 112-2221-E-007-079-MY3 and NSTC 113-2218-E-007-020.

However, this accuracy improvement has come at a cost
in terms of computational resources required for training and
inference [3]. ViT-based models have higher FLOPs compared
to equivalent CNN-based models, specially as the image size
is increased. This is due to the quadratic complexity O(N 2 )
of the self-attention mechanism with respect to the sequence
length N . Specialized FGIR models employing ViT as a
backbone [5] may even exhibit cubic complexity O(N 3 ) due
to their usage of aggregated attention [7].
Employing higher-resolution images in FGIR is common in
order to identify the small differences between classes [8], [9].
In order to effectively make use of current and next-generation
scaled models, specially in low-resource settings, it is crucial
to increase the efficiency of such ViT-based models; token
reduction is a promising direction to increase their efficiency.
Token Reduction (TR) proposes dropping [10], [11] or
combining [12], [13] unimportant tokens from the sequence
in order to reduce the number of tokens to be processed; it is
also known as token or input pruning. In computer vision these
tokens represent image patches. By reducing the number the
tokens the FLOPs, latency, and memory consumption could
be reduced, while throughput can be increased [10], [14].
Different TR methods use different criteria for selecting or
merging tokens; some use attention [11], [15], while others
employ learnable modules [10], [16]. However, regardless of
the TR selection criteria, the process of TR inevitably leads to
the loss of valuable information for discrimination, specially
as the keep ratio (KR) of tokens is reduced.
Therefore, to counteract the loss of information brought
by the TR process we propose two novel mechanisms: 1)
a Cross-Layer Aggregation (CLA) Classification Head which
incorporates skip connections for intermediate features of a
transformer directly into the classification module, and 2) a
Cross-Layer Cache (CLC) structure to store and recover crosslayer information vital for fine-grained classification.
We refer to our full method method, incorporating both the
CLA Head and the CLC, as Cross-Layer Cache Aggregation
(CLCA). CLCA allows us to push the boundary of TR
methods, by employing extremely low token keep ratios (KR)
of up to 10%, while keeping an accuracy that is on-par with (or
even surpass in certain cases) state-of-the-art (SotA) methods
in UFGIR. We demonstrate the performance improvement
brought by our proposed contributions with different token
KRs, and image sizes for the SoyGene dataset [2] in Fig. 1.

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Ultra-Fine-Grained Image Recognition
UFGIR methods make use of generic image recognition
backbones and equip them with modules to select and aggregate discriminative features [5], [6] or employ loss functions
and tasks [18] to guide models to more effectively make use
of fine-grained features.
However, many of these methods employ transformer-based
backbones with a large computational cost, specially as the
image size increases due to the quadratic complexity of the
self-attention operator.
B. Token Reduction
Token reduction methods can be broadly categorized into
token pruning and token merging. Token pruning involves the
dropping of a number of uninformative tokens at certain layers.

The overview of our proposed method is shown in Fig. 2.
Our method is based on a generic Vision Transformer with
token reduction. The transformer encoder blocks are divided
into four groups based on the token reduction locations and
the last layer. Features in each group are processed using
a series of encoder blocks followed by a token reduction
and cache recovery operations. We aggregate intermediate
features corresponding to the outputs of the CLS token of each
group. These features are first normalized, then cross-layer
channel-wise information is aggregated through a depth-wise
convolution. We incorporate a non-linear activation function
to model complex relations between features. Finally, a pointwise convolution projects the aggregated features into a vector
of classification predictions.
A. Vision Transformer Encoder Group with Token Reduction
Images are patchified using a convolution with kernel size
P and flattened into a 1D sequence of D channels with length
N = (S1 /P ) × (S2 /P ), where S1 and S2 represent the image
width and height. A learnable CLS token is appended at the
start of the sequence. Learnable positional embeddings are
added to incorporate spatial information.
This sequence is then processed through a series of L transformer encoder blocks, divided into g groups, each consisting
of multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and position-wise feedforward networks (PWFFN).
At the last encoder block of each group we reduce tokens
according to each TR scheme. Our method can be combined
with most token reduction methodologies but we focus on
EViT [15]. EViT selects tokens to keep based on the average
attention-score of the CLS token with respect to the other
tokens as computed in the MHSA block.
Specifically, the top-k tokens with highest attention based
on the CLS token attention A0 are kept and the remaining
tokens are merged using a weighted average operation. Then
the kept tokens and the fused token will be concatenated and
forwarded to the PWFFN block as normally.
B. Cross-Layer Aggregation (CLA) Head
To prevent information loss we fast-forward intermediate
features of the network directly into the classification layer.
Specifically, we forward the CLS token output of each encoder
group (after each reduction location and the last layer) into the
proposed CLA Head, shown in the right block of Fig. 2.
In the CLA Head we concatenate and reshape the tokens
into a sequence of size g, denoted as CLS ∈ RD×g , before

C. Cross-Layer Cache (CLC)
Regardless of the TR scheme, the information encoded in
the original tokens is lost. Since reduction is applied from
early layers that process low-level features, it is unavoidable
to lose information that deeper layers may require to make
a distinction between fine-grained categories. Therefore, inspired by FFVT, [6] which aggregated low-level and middlelevel features from the network in the last encoder layer of a
ViT for improved classification performance, we incorporate a
Cross-Layer Cache (CLC) structure to store local and discriminative features from intermediate layers for future usage. An
overview of the CLC is shown in the middle block of Fig. 2.
Specifically, after each transformer encoder block in an
encoder group, we store the Global-Average-Pooling (GAP)

pooled local features of the model. Furthermore, inspired
by previous work which suggested that transformers need
registers [20] we append a learnable Cross-Layer Register
(CLR) at the end of the sequence. After each encoder block we
also store this CLR that aggregates discriminative information
across layers for future use.
After the reduction location or before the last layer, we
access the CLC to recover the lost information tokens by
appending the tokens stored in it to the original sequence.
This allows our model to counteract the information loss that
comes with token reduction and make usage of relevant crosslayer information for discrimination of fine-grained categories.
Finally, the CLC is emptied so as to not repeat tokens in future
recovery operations.

Our experiments use 5 ultra-fine-grained leaves datasets
collected by Yu et al. [2] where each category represents a

confirmed cultivar name attached to the seed obtained from
the genetic resource bank. Top-1 accuracies are averaged
across three seeds [22]. When applicable, the best values are
highlighted in bold and the second best are underlined.
We employ AdamW optimizer, 50 epochs, weight decay
of 0.05, batch size of 32, two image sizes (224, 448), nine
backbones (ViT [4], DeiT [23], DeiT3 IN1k & IN21k [17],
MIIL [24], MoCov3 [25], DINO [26], MAE [27], CLIP
LAION [28]) five different keep rates (100, 70, 50, 25, and
10%). The token reduction operations are done on the 4th, 7th,
and 10th of a ViT B-16 model with 12 layers, therefore we
recover tokens from the CLC after these same locations and
also after layer 11.
The proposed CLCA is agnostic to the TR method. We
mainly apply it with EViT [15], but we also apply it to methods
from all four different TR paradigms: DynamicViT [10] for
static pruning, ATS for dynamic pruning [11], SiT [16] and
PatchMerger [12] for soft merging, and DPCKNN [19] and
ToMe [13] for hard merging.

B. Comparison with State-of-the-Art in UFGIR
We compare our method against state-of-the-art (SotA)
methods using ViTs in the Cotton, SoyAgeing, and SoyGlobal
datasets in Table I. When compared to these we observe that
our method obtains superior performance in terms of accuracy
in the Cotton and SoyAgeing datasets. For the SoyGlobal
dataset our method obtains the second best accuracy but the
computational cost in terms of FLOPs is much lower.

A. Comparison of CLCA with Different TR Schemes

Fig. 4. Ablation showing max magnitude of gradient across all layers of

proposed modules and the corresponding top-1 accuracy.

Fig. 3. Distribution of accuracies for multiple keep rates (25, 50, and 70%)

In Fig. 4 we plot the gradients during training for our model.
Our analysis reveals that, prior to incorporating the proposed
components, the gradients exhibit significant instability.
Therefore, inspired by previous which suggests that skip
connections can smooth out the optimization landscape [29]
we incorporate information transfer in between 1) intermediate
layers of the network to the (CLA) classification head, and 2)
intermediate layers of an encoder group and the subsequent
block through the usage of the Cross-Layer Cache (CLC).
These proposed modules function as skip connections which
have been shown to ease the training by avoiding spurious
local optimum [30] and encouraging feature reuse [31].
Furthermore, in UFGIR the usage of low-, middle-, and
high-level features has been shown to be beneficial to distinguish between classes [6]. Our proposed modules provide
shorter connections to these features, which has been shown
to provide implicit deep supervision [32].

We plot the accuracy vs FLOPs as a function of image
size (IS) and keep rate (KR) for the SoyGene dataset with
EViT in Fig. 1. By incorporating CLCA the lower bound of
the accuracy is raised to 61% and to a maximum of 81%
compared to the original design which ranged from 30% to
67%. Models with CLCA are more robust to the TR KR and
the IS compared to the original while incurring a negligible
increase in cost as measured by the FLOPs.
These results are further validated by plotting the accuracies
distribution (across 9 backbones, 3 keep rates, 3 random seeds,
on 2 datasets) for different TR schemes with and without
CLCA, as shown in Fig. 3. Our method consistently improves
the performance across a wide variety of settings.

VI. C ONCLUSION
In this paper we propose Cross-Layer Cache Aggregation
(CLCA), a method to improve the performance of token
reduction in ultra-fine-grained image recognition tasks. The
proposed method integrates intermediate features from the network through a Cross-Layer Aggregation (CLA) classification
head for robust predictions, and Cross-Layer Cache (CLC) to
recover and integrate lost information from previous layers
that is lost under the token reduction paradigm. The proposed
methodology can be combined with a variety of mechanisms
to reduce tokens, massively reducing the computational cost
while consistently boosting the performance across different
UFGIR datasets under a variety of settings.
